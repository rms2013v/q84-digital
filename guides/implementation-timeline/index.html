How long does a Business Central implementation take?

Typical timelines by project size, what happens week by week, and the decisions that keep a project on schedule. Updated November 24, 2026 · Q84 Digital consultants

PhasesDelaysGo-live dateIn depth
Book a free ERP assessment

The short answer: a focused Business Central rollout for a small or midsize company usually takes two to three months. Adding warehousing, an ERP migration or integrations stretches it to three to six months. Multi-company or manufacturing projects often run six months or more, usually in phases.

Project sizeTypical scopeTypical timeline
FocusedFinance, sales, purchasing and basic inventory, one company, straightforward data move2 to 3 months
StandardAdds warehouse processes, migration from another ERP, or one or two integrations3 to 6 months
ComplexSeveral companies, manufacturing, advanced warehousing or multiple integrations6 months or more, often phased
Phase by phase

Where the time goes in a standard project

PhaseTypical lengthWhat happens
Discovery2 to 4 weeksWorkshops by area, current processes and requirements
Design1 to 3 weeksRequirements mapped to standard features, apps or extensions; fixed scope agreed
Build4 to 10 weeksConfiguration, extensions and integrations, with data migration running alongside
Testing & training3 to 6 weeksYour team runs real processes end to end; role-based training
Go-live & first close2 to 4 weeksCutover, hands-on support and the first month-end in Business Central
What slows projects down

The usual causes of delay

  • Slow decisions. Questions waiting a week for an answer add up to months.
  • Team availability. Key users who can’t step away from daily work for workshops and testing.
  • Data cleanup left late. Dirty data found during the final load.
  • Scope added mid-project. New requirements after design is signed off.
  • Third parties. EDI partners, 3PLs or other vendors with their own schedules.
Choosing a go-live date

When to switch over

  • Start of a month or quarter. Opening balances are cleaner and reporting periods line up.
  • Avoid your busy season. Retailers and distributors usually avoid Q4.
  • Not the start of the year if you can help it. Year-end close and the go-live in the same weeks overload finance.
  • Leave a buffer. Two weeks of slack between final testing and go-live.
In depth

Example timeline: a 20-person distributor

Here’s how a standard project typically unfolds for a distributor moving from QuickBooks to Business Central with warehouse processes and one Shopify integration. Every project is different, but the shape is common.

WeeksWhat happens
1–3Discovery workshops for finance, sales, purchasing and warehouse. Data extracts start.
4–5Solution design signed off: chart of accounts, dimensions, posting groups, warehouse setup, integration design.
6–11Configuration, Shopify connector setup, document layouts, first trial data load.
12–15End-to-end testing by key users with migrated data, second trial load, training.
16Go-live at a month start: final data load and physical count over a weekend.
17–20Hands-on support and the first month-end close in Business Central.

What shortens a project

  • Clean data and inactive records removed before the project starts.
  • A decision-maker available for quick answers.
  • Accepting standard Business Central processes where they work.
  • Phasing: going live with the core first and adding extras later.

What usually needs more time

  • Manufacturing with routings and capacity planning.
  • Several companies with inter-company transactions and consolidation.
  • EDI with large retailers, because each trading partner tests and certifies separately.
  • Heavily customized source systems, such as older Dynamics NAV.

How to keep your project on schedule

Agree the go-live date at the start and work backwards. Book key users’ time for testing weeks in advance. Hold a short weekly status meeting with an open-issues list, and make sure every issue has an owner and a date. Most delays come from questions waiting for answers, not from the work itself.

Phasing a larger project

For bigger scopes, splitting the project into phases reduces risk and gets value sooner. A common pattern is:

  • Phase 1: finance, sales, purchasing and basic inventory for the main company.
  • Phase 2: warehouse processes and scanning, once the basics are stable.
  • Phase 3: integrations such as EDI or a web store, then additional companies or locations.

Each phase has its own go-live and a few weeks of settling in before the next starts. The total time is a little longer than one big-bang project, but the risk on any single weekend is much lower and your team learns the system in stages.

Questions to ask about any proposed timeline

  • How many hours a week does the plan assume from our key users?
  • When are the trial data loads, and who reconciles them?
  • What has to be true before go-live is approved?
  • What happens to the timeline if a decision takes a week instead of a day?

Timelines for migrations from older systems

Where you’re coming from changes the shape of the project. Moves from QuickBooks or spreadsheets spend more time on designing item data and inventory processes, because those didn’t really exist before. Moves from Dynamics GP benefit from Microsoft’s migration tools but need time to rethink account segments and replace add-ons. Moves from Dynamics NAV are often driven by how much custom code needs replacing.

After go-live

Plan for a settling-in period of one to three months after go-live. Users find faster ways of working, small setup changes are made, and reports are fine-tuned. Many companies then start a second phase for the features they deliberately left out of the first one.

A note on year-end

Going live at the start of a fiscal year is tempting because it gives a clean break, but it lands on top of year-end close, audit preparation and tax filings. Many finance teams prefer a go-live a month or two into the new year, once the year-end work is done, with opening balances taken from the closed year. Discuss the timing with your accountant early.

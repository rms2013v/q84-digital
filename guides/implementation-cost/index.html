How much does a Business Central implementation cost?

An honest breakdown of what you’ll pay, where the money goes and what makes projects bigger or smaller. Updated November 22, 2026 · Q84 Digital consultants

RangesExampleDriversSaving moneyIn depthFAQ
Book a free ERP assessment

The short answer: for small and midsize USCanadian businesses, a Business Central implementation is typically a one-time project in the tens of thousands of dollars, plus monthly Microsoft licenses. A focused finance and inventory rollout sits at the lower end. Multi-company, manufacturing or integration-heavy projects can reach six figures. The rest of this guide explains what drives those numbers.

Cost drivers

What makes a project bigger

  • Manufacturing. Bills of materials, routings and costing add design and testing time, and need Premium.
  • Warehousing. Bins, directed picking and scanning add setup, devices and training.
  • Several companies. Each entity needs setup; inter-company and consolidation add more.
  • Integrations. Each connected system adds design, build and testing.
  • Messy data. Cleaning duplicate customers and inconsistent items takes real time.
  • Custom development. Every extension must be specified, built, tested and maintained.
Keeping costs down

How to spend less without cutting corners

  • Start with standard features. Adapt processes to Business Central where it costs you nothing to do so.
  • Phase the project. Go live with finance and inventory first, then add warehousing or integrations.
  • Clean data early. Start deleting duplicates and inactive records before the project starts.
  • Name an owner. One person who can make decisions quickly saves weeks.
  • Use the readiness checklist. Our free checklist surfaces scope gaps before they become change orders.
In depth

Budgeting beyond year one

Year one vs the years after

The first year carries the one-time implementation, data migration and integration costs on top of licenses. From year two, the budget is mostly licenses, app subscriptions and support. When comparing Business Central with other systems, compare three to five years of total cost rather than the first invoice.

Hidden costs to ask about

  • Your team’s time. Key users spend real hours in workshops, testing and training. Plan for backfill in busy periods.
  • Data cleanup. If the data is in poor shape, someone has to clean it, either your team or the consultant.
  • Reports. Custom invoice, packing slip and label layouts are often forgotten in early estimates.
  • Hardware. Scanners, label printers and Wi-Fi upgrades for warehouse scanning.
  • Change orders. Requirements added after the scope is signed.

Fixed price or time and materials?

A fixed price after discovery gives budget certainty for a clearly defined scope, and is the norm for well-scoped small and midsize projects. Time and materials suits work where the scope genuinely can’t be known yet, such as a rescue or a complex integration. Many projects combine both: fixed price for the core implementation, time and materials for later improvements.

Questions to ask any implementation partner

  • What exactly is included, and what is excluded?
  • How many trial data loads are included?
  • Who on their team will do the work, and how senior are they?
  • How are changes to scope priced and approved?
  • What support is included after go-live, and for how long?

How to compare two quotes

Implementation quotes are only comparable when the scope is the same. Line them up against the same list: modules, companies, locations, number of trial data loads, integrations, custom reports, training sessions and post-go-live support. A cheaper quote often leaves out items the other includes, which come back later as change orders.

Finally, keep a contingency of around 10 to 15 percent for genuine surprises, such as a data problem nobody knew about or a new requirement from a key customer.

Revisit the budget at each phase gate, so surprises are caught early rather than at go-live.

FAQ

Cost questions

Have a different question? Ask directly.

Why don’t you publish a fixed price?

Because two companies with the same number of users can need very different projects. One may need finance and basic inventory; another needs warehousing, manufacturing and three integrations. We give a fixed quote after discovery, once we know which one you are.

What’s the most common reason budgets grow?

Scope that wasn’t written down. Requirements that surface late, such as a forgotten integration or a report finance relies on, are the biggest driver of overruns.

Can we lower the cost by doing some work ourselves?

Yes. Teams that clean their own data, write their own test scripts and train their own users after a train-the-trainer session can reduce consulting time noticeably.

Common problems

Why ERP projects go sideways

Most failed implementations fail for the same few reasons. Our method is built to prevent each one.

  • Vague scope. Nobody agrees on what “done” means, so the budget keeps moving.
  • Dirty data. Old records are loaded as-is and cause problems from day one.
  • Too much customization. Custom code replaces standard features and makes upgrades harder.
  • Rushed testing. Real processes aren’t tested end to end before go-live.
  • Untrained users. People go back to spreadsheets because nobody showed them how.

In depth

What a well-run Business Central implementation looks like

Most Business Central projects don’t fail because of the software. They fail because of decisions that were made too late, data that was never cleaned, or a team that was never really involved. A good implementation partner spends as much time on those risks as on configuration.

Decisions to make before configuration starts

A handful of early design choices shape everything that follows, and they are expensive to change after go-live:

  • Chart of accounts and dimensions. Which reporting lives in the account number and which lives in dimensions such as department, location or channel. Getting this right is what makes Power BI and financial reports simple later.
  • Posting groups. How customers, vendors and items post to the general ledger. Most “the numbers don’t match” problems trace back here.
  • Inventory costing method. FIFO, average or standard cost. It can’t be changed casually once items have transactions.
  • Warehouse setup per location. Simple shipping from a single location, bins, or full directed put-away and pick. More control means more steps for the warehouse team.
  • Number series and document flow. Quote to order to shipment to invoice, and who approves what along the way.

Who you need on your side

Business Central is implemented with your team, not for it. You need a project owner who can make decisions within a day or two, and a key user for each area: finance, sales, purchasing and warehouse. Key users join the workshops, test their own processes and train their colleagues. Budget around a day a week from each of them during the build and testing phases, and more in the final weeks before go-live.

Testing that actually finds problems

Testing a few screens is not enough. Before go-live your team should run complete business scenarios end to end: a customer order from quote to cash, a purchase from requisition to vendor payment, a stock count, a return, and a month-end close. Each scenario uses migrated data, not demo data, so data problems surface while there is still time to fix them.

Cutover and the first month

Go-live is usually timed for the start of a month. The final data load happens over a weekend: closing balances, open documents and a physical stock count. The first two weeks are the most intensive; we stay close to the team while they post real transactions, then support the first month-end close in Business Central. After that, the project moves into ongoing support at whatever level you need.

Signs a partner is a good fit

  • They ask about your processes before they talk about features.
  • They tell you which requests to handle with standard features instead of custom code.
  • They put scope, assumptions and exclusions in writing.
  • The person who scopes the project is involved in delivering it.

Seven signs your distribution business has outgrown QuickBooks

November 16, 2026

QuickBooks is where most distributors start, and for good reason: it’s affordable, familiar and good at basic accounting. But distribution is an inventory business, and QuickBooks was built for accounting first. Somewhere between a few hundred and a few thousand SKUs, the workarounds start to cost more than the software saves. Here are the seven signs we see most often.

1. Your real inventory lives in a spreadsheet

If the stock numbers your team trusts are in Excel rather than QuickBooks, your books are only as accurate as the last time someone copied numbers across. Every manual update is a chance to oversell, under-order or misstate inventory value on the balance sheet. (Here’s how spreadsheet data moves into an ERP.)

2. You stock more than one location

A second warehouse, a 3PL or even a van stock location changes everything. You need to know what’s where, move stock between locations and pick from the right place. QuickBooks can track locations in its higher editions, but bins, transfers and location-level replenishment quickly push past what it was designed for.

3. Purchasing runs on memory

When buyers reorder based on gut feel or a weekly spreadsheet review, you end up with too much of what doesn’t sell and not enough of what does. A distribution ERP suggests purchase orders from reorder points, lead times and actual demand.

4. You can’t see true margin

Freight, duty and handling change what a product really costs you. If landed costs never reach the item, your margin reports are optimistic, and some of your best-selling products may be your least profitable.

5. Month-end takes a week

If finance spends days exporting, reconciling and rebuilding reports in Excel, the system isn’t doing its job. Inventory value that doesn’t match the general ledger is the classic symptom.

6. Orders are typed more than once

A web order retyped for the warehouse, then again for invoicing, costs time and causes errors. When orders come from Shopify, EDI or email, they should flow into one system automatically.

7. Your team has outgrown the file

Slow company files, user limits, thin permissions and no approval workflows are signs the business has grown past a small-business tool. Growth should make your systems more useful, not more fragile.

If three or more of these sound familiar, it’s time to look at an ERP, not another spreadsheet.

Is it QuickBooks, or is it your processes?

Before blaming the software, it’s worth checking. Some problems follow you to any system: no one owns the item list, receipts aren’t entered on the day goods arrive, or counts are never reconciled. A new ERP won’t fix those on its own, but it does make them visible and gives you the tools to enforce better habits, such as required fields, approval workflows and posted entries that can’t be quietly edited.

A useful test: if your team follows a consistent process and still needs spreadsheets to get answers, the system is the bottleneck. If the process itself varies from person to person, fix that first, ideally as part of the ERP project.

What about QuickBooks Enterprise or add-on apps?

Many distributors try to stretch QuickBooks first, with QuickBooks Enterprise’s advanced inventory or a separate inventory app synced to QuickBooks Online. That can buy a year or two. The trade-offs show up as the business grows: two systems to reconcile, sync errors to chase, reporting split across tools, and limits on users, locations or transaction volume. When you find yourself adding a third or fourth app to cover gaps, you’re effectively building an ERP out of parts, without the single database that makes an ERP worth having.

What moving to an ERP actually involves

For a small or midsize distributor, a move from QuickBooks to an ERP such as Business Central usually takes two to six months, depending on how many locations, integrations and warehouse processes are in scope. The main steps are:

  • Discovery: how you buy, stock, sell, ship and close the month today.
  • Design: chart of accounts, dimensions, item setup and warehouse processes in the new system.
  • Data migration: customers, vendors, items, open invoices and bills, and opening balances, with stock from a physical count.
  • Testing and training: your team runs real orders end to end before go-live.
  • Go-live: usually at the start of a month, with support through the first close.

Most of your QuickBooks history doesn’t need to move. Keep QuickBooks available for lookups, and bring open items, balances and summary history into the new system. See how long implementation takes for a week-by-week example.

What it costs

An ERP costs more than QuickBooks, both in monthly licenses and in a one-time implementation. The return comes from the problems above: fewer stockouts and overstock, faster month-end, less retyping and fewer costly mistakes. Questions to ask before choosing a replacement

  • Does it track stock by location and bin, with costs that flow to the general ledger?
  • Can it suggest purchase orders from reorder points, lead times and open orders?
  • How does it handle landed costs such as freight and duty?
  • Can it take orders from your web store and EDI customers without retyping?
  • Can your team use Excel and Outlook with it?
  • Who will implement and support it, and have they moved other distributors off QuickBooks?

A system that answers yes to most of these will remove the workarounds that QuickBooks forces on a growing distributor. A partner who has done the move before will help you avoid the data problems that make migrations painful: duplicate customers, inconsistent units of measure and inventory values that don’t match the books.

How long can you wait?

There’s rarely a perfect moment to change systems, but there are bad ones. Avoid starting an ERP project right before your busiest season, and avoid going live in the middle of it. Most distributors plan to go live in a quieter month, which means starting the project three to six months earlier. If you can see the signs above today, the cost of waiting another year is usually more stockouts, more overstock and more hours lost to reconciling, at a time when the business is larger and the move is harder.

Making the case internally

Owners and finance leaders usually want the business case in numbers. Estimate the hours spent each month on retyping orders, reconciling inventory and rebuilding reports; the cost of stockouts and emergency freight; and the stock written off or discounted because nobody knew it was there. Even rough figures usually show that the workarounds already cost more than the system would. Pair that with the risks of staying put: key-person dependency on whoever maintains the spreadsheets, and growth that the current setup can’t support.

Who should be involved

An ERP decision isn’t only finance’s. Involve whoever runs purchasing, the warehouse and sales from the start; they will use the system every day and know where today’s workarounds are. A small selection team of three or four people, with one clear decision-maker, usually works better than a large committee.

Common mistakes when leaving QuickBooks

  • Bringing all the history. Years of transactions slow the project and rarely get used. Bring open items, balances and summary history; keep QuickBooks for lookups.
  • Copying the old chart of accounts. A move is the best chance to simplify it and use dimensions instead of dozens of similar accounts.
  • Skipping the physical count. Opening stock should come from a count, not from the spreadsheet everyone already distrusts.
  • Training too early. Train people in the last few weeks before go-live, on their own data, so it sticks.

What to do next

Start by writing down what hurts most and what “better” would look like: accurate stock by location, faster month-end, fewer typed orders. Then look at systems built for distribution. Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central is a common next step for QuickBooks users because it adds real inventory, warehousing and purchasing while keeping finance familiar and working with Microsoft 365.

Moving is less disruptive than most companies expect. Customers, vendors, items, open invoices and balances come across; the old transaction history usually stays in QuickBooks for reference.
